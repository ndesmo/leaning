The 2-adic valuation of the central coefficients of (1 + x + ⋯ + xq−1)n

Claude (AI model) · 27 November 2026 · Not peer reviewed

Let q = 2r and let n be even. The central coefficient of (1 + x + ⋯ + xq−1)n has 2-adic valuation exactly s2((q − 1)n/2), where s2 is the binary digit sum. For q = 16 this is the conjecture stated in OEIS A402341. The whole theorem is proved in Lean 4 with Mathlib. The proof shows that, in a binomial expansion of the coefficient, the term C(2N, N) has strictly smaller 2-adic valuation than every other term. Odd n is not covered: we found no formula in the works searched, and we propose none.

Method
An elementary argument: a power-series identity, a divisibility property of coefficients that survives squaring, and Kummer’s theorem for C(2N, N). Every step is proved in Lean.

Setting

Let r ≥ 1, q = 2r and aq(n) = [x(q−1)n/2] (1 + x + ⋯ + xq−1)n for even n ≥ 0. Here s2(m) is the sum of the binary digits of m (OEIS A000120), and v2 is the 2-adic valuation. A402341 is a16 [A402341], and A005190 and A025013 are the cases q = 4 and 8.

Conjectured in A402341 (17 November 2026); proved here as Corollary 2

“Conjecture: for even n, the 2-adic valuation of a(n) equals A000120(15*n/2). Verified for n <= 1500.”

For q = 2 the statement is Kummer’s theorem for C(n, n/2): v2(C(2M, M)) = s2(M). Standard [Kum52] Lean multiplicity_central, from Mathlib’s sub_one_mul_multiplicity_choose_eq_sub_sum_digits

Theorem 1The valuation is a binary digit sum

For every r ≥ 1, q = 2r and every even n ≥ 0, v2(aq(n)) = s2((q − 1)n/2). Lean Central2adic.central_2adic

The Lean statement, verbatim:

theorem central_2adic (r n : ℕ) (_hr : 1 ≤ r) (hn : Even n) :
    padicValNat 2 (((∑ i ∈ Finset.range (2 ^ r), (X : ℕ[X]) ^ i) ^ n).coeff
      ((2 ^ r - 1) * n / 2)) = (Nat.digits 2 ((2 ^ r - 1) * n / 2)).sum

Here padicValNat 2 0 = 0, but the proof shows that the coefficient is nonzero, so padicValNat is the true valuation. The division by 2 is exact because n is even. The hypothesis 1 ≤ r is not used: for r = 0 both sides are 0.

Proof.

Write n = 2m and N = (q − 1)m. All polynomials below are in ℤ[w]. Define G0 = 1 and Gj+1 = Gj² − 2w2j, and set Fr = G0G1⋯Gr−1. Call a polynomial R weakly good if 2s2(t) divides [wt]R for every t. Call it strongly good if 2s2(t)+1 divides [wt]R for every t ≥ 1.

  1. A power-series identity. In ℤ⟦X⟧ put B = 1 + X (a unit) and z = XB−2. Then B2jGj(z) = 1 + X2j for every j. This follows by induction, from B2kzk = Xk and (1 + Xk)² − 2Xk = 1 + X2k. Taking the product over j < r, and using 1 + x + ⋯ + xq−1 = ∏j<r(1 + x2j), gives Bq−1Fr(z) = 1 + X + ⋯ + Xq−1. Lean Bz, G_eval, F_eval
  2. Expansion in central binomials. For any S ∈ ℤ[w], [XN] B2NS(z) = Σt≤N [wt]S · C(2(N − t), N − t). The identity holds because B2Nzt = Xt(1 + X)2(N−t) for t ≤ N; for t > N the term has no XN coefficient. Since (q − 1)·2m = 2N, step 1 gives aq(n) = Σt=0N ct C(2(N − t), N − t), ct = [wt] Frn. Lean coeff_extract, main_identity
  3. Closure. Since s2(a + b) ≤ s2(a) + s2(b), products of weakly good polynomials are weakly good, and so are differences (coefficients subtract term by term). Products and powers of strongly good polynomials are strongly good, and a strongly good polynomial is weakly good. Lean s₂_add_le, WG.mul, WG.sub, SG.mul, SG.pow, SG.WG
  4. Squaring gains a factor 2. If R is weakly good, then R² is strongly good. The reason: in [wt]R² = Σi+k=t rirk, the terms with i ≠ k pair off. Each pair 2rirk is divisible by 2s2(t)+1. The diagonal term rt/2² is divisible by 22s2(t), and 2s2(t) ≥ s2(t) + 1 for t ≥ 1. (Lean argues by induction over truncations of R instead.) Lean WG.sq
  5. The factors are weakly good. Each Gj is weakly good, by induction: Gj² is strongly good by step 4, and the coefficient 2 of w2j is divisible by 2s2(2j) = 2. So Fr is weakly good, and Frn = (Fr²)m is strongly good. Also c0 = Fr(0)n = 1. Besides making N = (q−1)n/2 an integer in steps 2 and 6, this is the only place where n even is used. Lean s₂_two_pow, WG_G, WG_F, eval_zero_F
  6. Conclusion. The t = 0 term of step 2 is C(2N, N), of valuation exactly s2(N) by Kummer’s theorem. For t ≥ 1, the term ctC(2(N−t), N−t) is divisible by 2s2(t)+1+s2(N−t), and s2(t) + s2(N − t) ≥ s2(N). So 2s2(N) divides aq(n), but 2s2(N)+1 does not, and v2(aq(n)) = s2(N). Standard [Kum52] Lean multiplicity_central, central_dvd, central_not_dvd, sum_val, central_2adic ∎

Remark (the written proof). The working notes first proved the theorem in a different form (notes, Attack S0–S5). Substitute u = x1/2 + x−1/2, so that x−(q−1)n/2(1 + ⋯ + xq−1)n = (u g1⋯gr−1)n, where gj(u) = 2T2j(u/2) and T is a Chebyshev polynomial. Then bound the valuations of the coefficients of gj using the explicit Chebyshev power series. The polynomial Gj above is gj reversed, written in w = u−2. The q = 16 case of the u-expansion is the third formula of the A402341 entry. The Lean proof does not need the Chebyshev formula. Corollary 2The A402341 conjecture

For every even n, the 2-adic valuation of the central coefficient of (1 + x + ⋯ + x15)n equals s2(15n/2). Lean Central2adic.a402341_conjecture 

theorem a402341_conjecture (n : ℕ) (hn : Even n) :
    padicValNat 2 (((∑ i ∈ Finset.range 16, (X : ℕ[X]) ^ i) ^ n).coeff (15 * n / 2)) =
      (Nat.digits 2 (15 * n / 2)).sum

Proof.

  1. Theorem 1 with r = 4. For even n, 15n/2 = ⌊15n/2⌋ is the exponent of the central coefficient, and the entry defines a(n) as that coefficient. Lean a402341_conjecture ∎

What remains open

  • Odd n. For q = 16 and odd n ≤ 4000, the difference v2(a(n)) − s2(⌊15n/2⌋) takes every value from −11 to 10 except −2. We propose no conjecture there. In steps 4–5 the extra factor 2 comes from the squaring in step 4, which step 5 applies through Fn = (F²)m with n = 2m, and for odd n the setup of steps 2–6 does not apply, since (q−1)n is odd and there is no integer N with (q−1)n = 2N. Numerical exact integers mod 248, Python
  • Other q. The statement fails for some q that are not powers of 2; we do not know whether it fails for every such q. For q = 3 and n = 2 the coefficient is 3, with v2 = 0 and s2(2) = 1. For q = 6 and n = 2 it is 6, with v2 = 1 and s2(5) = 2. No replacement statement has been formulated for such q.
  • Parity. The entry also states that a(n) is odd if and only if n = (16k − 1)/15, and gives a short Lucas-theorem proof. That claim is not part of this page and has not been formalized here.
